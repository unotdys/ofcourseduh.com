Defying Entropy: Mike Johnson on the Group 42 CD, Anchor ID and AI Attribution

29:56 · Open on YouTube

From a hacker CD-ROM in the mid-'90s to a UUID that could stop AI from forgetting who made what: Mike Johnson's backstory.

In this episode of Remotely Local, remote.it co-founders Ryo Koyama and Mike Johnson, who have worked together at six companies and founded three, talk about attribution in the age of AI. Mike goes back to the Group 42 CD, a 1994–95 information archive his group built in HTML so you could browse it like the web. It was pressed in 2,000 copies at $1.98 each and sold for $49 plus shipping, cash to a PO box. That project gave him his "defy entropy" mantra: keep building, keep preserving, don't let things disappear. He also tells how the tiny open-source TCP/IP stack he wrote for an AVR microcontroller still turns up in 88 GitHub repos. Then he explains why he built Anchor ID, an open-source way to tie all of your work and online identities to a single UUID so your work stays attributed to you when AI trains on it.

Along the way Ryo argues that AI is becoming a kind of religion, where nobody knows who wrote what, and that its training data is this generation's history books. Mike explains why Anchor ID is deliberately not an identity, a crypto token or a blockchain, and how it keeps working even if his own resolver disappears. They compare Eastern and Western ideas of leaving something behind. For the AI hot take, Mike calls this an echo of the '90s internet boom, only orders of magnitude bigger. Ryo counters that social media plus AI may be the greatest war machine in history. They finish on gatekeepers, federation, and why Mike hosts his own blog.

Making Music with AI: Mike Johnson on a Playa Gift, Chronosonics and a Clear Lake Album

18:51 · Open on YouTube

From a Burning Man playa gift to a Clear Lake concept album sung in his own voice: Mike Johnson on making music with AI.

In this episode of Remotely Local, remote.it co-founders Ryo Koyama and Mike Johnson talk about Mike's accidental music career. It started with Suno "back in the 3.0 days" and a question: what to give people at Burning Man 2025? His answer was an album. The Reared In Steel crew wrote the lyrics together in a shared Google Doc, and Mike handed out the album on 75 USB necklaces, taking one off his own neck whenever he met someone interesting. Then came Chronosonics, his habit of turning each blog post into a song so readers remember the ideas. After that he made The North Shore Kind of Heaven, an Americana concept album about 22 summers at the family's Clear Lake house. His wife wrote two of the songs. He recorded his own voice as the baseline and ran ACE-Step 1.5, a local version of Suno, on one of his 3090 cards. A skeptical producer, one of Mike's old Eagle Scouts, came round: "This is not what I thought."

Ryo argues that AI music is the opposite of vibe coding. Instead of letting people skip a skill they could learn, it lets people who never trained in music render what's in their head. The two remember "A Pirate's Life for Me," a parody Mike once recorded with his kids. Mike tells the story of "Konocti Sunset," a song about boating eight minutes across the lake to concerts until the venue went dark. They finish on why recording memories fights entropy, and why Ryo tells parents to give their kids the best camera they can buy.

Bringing AI to Batting Practice with Jignesh Patel of BatFast

22:41 · Open on YouTube

What do a Kenyan cricket dream, a hacked Australian pitching machine and a Honda Civic have to do with physical AI?

Ryo Koyama of remote.it talks with Jignesh Patel of BatFast, the company behind operator-less batting simulators for cricket, baseball, tennis and lacrosse. You face a projected bowler or pitcher, a ball machine hidden behind the screen delivers the ball, and ball tracking (with pose tracking in development) turns every shot into performance data. Jignesh tells how BatFast started: his co-founder saw Topgolf and a baseball pitching machine in Dubai and wanted the same for cricket. The quote for a machine was too expensive, so the two of them bought one from Australia, reverse-engineered it with a new PCB and control system, and took it to a cricket academy in Leicester. A parent's request to bring it to a kid's birthday party became the business. The machine's release height still comes from the fact that it had to fit in his Honda Civic.

Along the way: how a portable system had more than 100 events booked within a year, why COVID turned out to be a blessing in disguise, and how BatFast now runs over 280 systems in more than 24 countries, managing them remotely with remote.it. Jignesh explains why one hardware stack can serve several sports, how machine learning started with getting the machines to deliver repeatable balls, and how tools like Claude and ChatGPT have democratized the analysis of BatFast's huge data set. Ryo adds why connecting legacy COBOL and serial equipment to an LLM is suddenly easy. For the AI hot take, Jignesh is "insanely optimistic": AI will democratize knowledge, but it has no taste, so humans still have to supply it.

Data Centers in Your Backyard with Josh Mustill of SPAN

22:19 · Open on YouTube

What if the next data center wasn't in the desert, but in your neighbor's backyard?

Ryo Koyama and Mike Johnson of remote.it visit SPAN in San Francisco to talk with Josh Mustill. SPAN makes a smart electrical panel that replaces the hundred-year-old breaker box with an internet-connected, remotely controllable one. Josh explains how that panel gives homeowners, home builders and utilities live visibility into how electricity is actually used, from a single home up to a whole substation. He also walks through Xfra, SPAN's new project with NVIDIA that puts micro data centers next to homes and runs them on excess solar that would otherwise be sold back to the grid for pennies.

Along the way: what remote work did to EV charging, why NEM 3.0 makes local compute interesting, how you secure a data center bolted to a slab in someone's garden, and whether "not in my backyard" applies to AI. Josh also covers why SPAN keeps AI on a short leash around customer hardware, how SPAN has used remote.it for nearly eight years to fix panels in the field from San Francisco, and his hot take on what happens when AI stops being a free lunch.
